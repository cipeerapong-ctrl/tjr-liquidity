## P13.1 What liquidity is and why it matters
- P13.1 Liquidity = resting orders on the chart. (02:00–02:46)
- P13.2 The market needs orders to be filled in order to move; with no orders being filled it goes sideways. (02:58–03:19)
- P13.3 A trader wants to know where those orders sit, because the goal is to predict, with high probability and daily, where price will go. (03:39–04:20)
- P13.4 He calls liquidity the centre of his strategy: it begins with liquidity and ends with liquidity. (04:20–04:51)

## P13.2 Where the orders sit — retail view
- P13.5 Builds on the previous episode: uptrend = higher highs and higher lows, downtrend = lower highs and lower lows. (05:13–05:34)
- P13.6 In an uptrend many people plan to press buy when price pushes above a high, betting on a higher high; he says he does NOT want students to do that and promises to explain why later. (06:07–06:48)
- P13.7 Those buyers put their stop-loss under the low, because in a true uptrend a higher low should hold; the low is their invalidation point. (07:42–08:58)
- P13.8 Result: buy orders rest above highs and sell orders rest below lows. (09:28–09:51)
- P13.9 Downtrend is the mirror: sellers enter on a break of a low and put stop-losses above highs. (10:02–11:05)
- P13.10 Two sets of resting orders above a high: new buyers entering on the break, plus shorts being stopped out (a stop-loss on a short is a buy order). Same two sets, reversed, under a low. (11:15–12:43, 14:57–15:49, 16:24–17:21)
- P13.11 Summary line: orders lie above highs and below lows. (12:55–13:28)

## P13.3 Retail vs smart money
- P13.12 This is how the retail trader thinks; 99% of traders fail, and he says that is meant to be used as an advantage, not as discouragement. He wants students to think like the market makers. (17:33–18:03)
- P13.13 Do not press buy because a high broke or sell because a low broke; that is how the majority trades, and he wants the opposite view. (18:03–18:26)
- P13.14 Market makers need a large number of orders in the OPPOSITE direction to fill their own large orders (example with 10,000 contracts needing 1,000 on the other side, simplified by him). (19:01–21:03)
- P13.15 To get them, they push price above highs: shorts get stopped out and new buyers enter, which gives the market makers buy-side liquidity to sell into; then price falls. He calls it reverse psychology: retail is faked into thinking price goes higher. (21:13–22:54)
- P13.16 Same in reverse under lows: buyers are stopped out, new sellers enter, and the market makers fill their buy orders and push price up. (23:04–23:55)
- P13.17 Warning in his own words: sell orders under lows do not mean you should sell; look in the opposite direction. He promises how to trade this later in the series ("I'm not going to tell you how to take trades yet"). (24:06–24:51)

## P13.4 Live chart examples

- P13.19 Example down-through-lows: price goes under lows, buyers stopped, new sellers enter, then price rises. (26:10–26:53)

- P13.22 Examples of sweeps at large scale: the tariff flash crash and February 2020 on higher time frames, both framed as buying opportunities after lows were swept. (28:33–30:08)

## P13.5 Caution and homework
- P13.23 Do NOT take away "sell every break of a high, buy every break of a low": trends do exist, and liquidity being swept is how new trends form. (30:55–31:26)
- P13.24 A sweep only gives market makers the OPPORTUNITY to change the trend; it does not mean price reverses every time. Chart walkthrough on NQ1!: several lows were taken and price kept falling before a reversal finally held. (31:37–32:42, 33:58)
- P13.25 First step of his strategy: focus on catching the bottoms of reversals up and the tops of reversals down. (32:53–33:16)
- P13.26 He says confirmation from other confluences is needed to know the orders are really being filled by the market makers; that comes later in the series, and he does not say what those confluences are here. (34:19–34:42)
- P13.27 Homework: find five examples of price pushing above a high then reversing, and five of pushing under a low then reversing. Do not yet try to explain why one high reversed and another did not. (34:42–35:27)
