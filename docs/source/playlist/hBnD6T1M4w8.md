## P2.1 The three skills and what "psychology" means to him
- P2.1 A profitable trader needs three skill sets: strategy, risk management, psychology. (0:21–0:31)
- P2.2 On strategy he says the "bare bones" are done: the confluences he uses have been taught and daily bias has been covered. What is still missing is how he puts them together to execute; that is promised for the next (last) episode. He does risk management first because viewers tend to take the strategy and leave. (0:31–1:03)
- P2.3 Psychology, to him, simply means sticking to two things: the trading plan and the risk-management plan. (1:03–1:23)
- P2.4 He frames it as discipline: only take a trade when the strategy presents itself, do not over-risk, do not over-leverage, do not over-trade. (1:47–2:08)
- P2.5 Failing to follow the trading plan = bad psychology; failing to follow the risk plan = bad psychology. He says good psychology comes automatically if you stick to the other two skills. (2:08–2:30)
- P2.6 He says he has no exercise, homework or motivational tip that improves psychology; it depends only on whether the trader wants to be profitable. (2:42–3:12)
- P2.7 He admits sticking to both is harder than it sounds because people are emotionally attached to money. (3:36–3:48)
- P2.8 His only psychological advice: if you want it badly enough you will stick to your strategy and your risk plan, because he sees no other route to profitability. (3:58–4:20)
- P2.9 Psychology is "massive" and is why most people do not turn profitable, but he says it is not something to work on or grow; it is a yes/no decision to follow the two plans. (5:35–6:08)
- P2.10 Get-rich-quick thinking breaks both plans: it leads to taking more trades outside the strategy and raising leverage and risk every day. (6:19–6:51)
- P2.11 He says trading will not make you rich within months; you build the skill first, then build capital over time, then collect the rewards. (6:51–7:45)
- P2.12 His claim, unverified: he only recently began "reaping the rewards" of trading; 2–3 years earlier he traded smaller size and earned far less; he calls it years of work and says viewers should not expect his position by the end of the year. (7:45–8:38)
- P2.13 Being honest with yourself means accepting it takes a long time, focusing on the skill of trading, sticking to a strategy that gives a probability, and using a risk plan so the account is not blown ("live to trade another day"); after that it is slow stacking. (8:48–9:18)
- P2.14 Funded accounts (covered in an earlier episode) can help low-capital traders scale faster; he does not say scaling quickly is impossible, only that it takes time. (9:18–9:38)

## P2.2 Strategy + risk plan + statistics
- P2.15 He calls trading simple: find a strategy that gives a probability, then a risk plan that can sustain that strategy given its win rate and its risk-to-reward, so a losing streak does not blow the account. (9:59–10:19)
- P2.16 Journal and statistics: if data in a trading journal (he names TradeZella as an example) shows a positive win rate and a positive risk-to-reward, then the strategy has a proven probability and the trader should stick to it and use correct risk. He gives no target number for win rate or risk-to-reward here. (4:40–5:01)
- P2.17 Risk should be a consistent amount that would not blow the account after a few losses in a row, nor after a losing month or two losing months in a row. (5:13–5:35)

## P2.3 His own risk sizing (live account)
- P2.18 Earlier in his career he calculated a fixed percentage of the balance (1% per trade) each time; he says the right percentage differs by each person's risk tolerance. (10:31–10:42)
- P2.19 Now he risks a fixed number of contracts per trade instead of a percentage: the same contracts every time he presses buy or sell on Nasdaq, and a set (different) number on ES. He does this so he does not need a lot-size or contract-size calculator. (10:42–11:17)
- P2.20 He recommends viewers on a live account do the same: find a contract count that is a safe amount for the account balance. (11:17–11:38)
- P2.21 He says that does not mean risking 5%, 10% or 20% of the balance per trade; he calls 1–3% "usually the sweet spot". (11:27–11:49)
- P2.22 To choose the count, look at your typical stop-loss size in ticks on past trades. His examples: a winning ES trade that day had a stop around 16 ticks; another S&P short had 34 ticks; another about 28. (11:49–12:20) Frames confirm the first two: the ES1! 1-minute chart shows a short position box whose label reads stop 4.00 points = 16 ticks, and a second box with stop 8.50 points = 34 ticks. The 28-tick example is not visible on screen.
- P2.23 Worked reasoning: the trade with the tight stop is the "bare minimum" loss at his count (he says about three contracts); then check what the loss would be at the widest typical stop (about 35 ticks) with that same count; the number only matters if he is comfortable with the loss at the widest typical stop. (12:20–12:55)
- P2.24 His estimate: a 35-tick stop could be about 3–4% of the account, and he says he accepts that. With only a 1:1 risk-to-reward that is roughly a 3% gain if it wins and a 3% loss if it stops out. (13:06–13:27)
- P2.25 On a tighter stop with a higher risk-to-reward he risks less but, in his reasoning, can make as much or more (no statistics shown); the full-take-profit trade he cites was 1:4.81. He says using the same contract size makes it "even out" over time. (13:27–14:00) Frames confirm: the 16-tick short box is labelled closed, risk/reward 4.81, with a target of 77 ticks (19.25 points); the 34-tick box he drags beside it shows risk/reward about 0.97 with a 33-tick target, i.e. roughly 1:1.
- P2.26 Exceptions where he changes size: (a) the stop-loss is drastically larger than usual, then he halves the contracts; (b) days with fundamental/news data (his example: PPI) where he expects choppier markets or does not like the look of it, then he also halves the contracts. (14:09–14:41)
- P2.27 On-screen detail (frames only, no spoken comment): the position boxes on the chart are TradingView's own long/short tool, dated Fri 16 Jan '26 (the 16-tick short spans about 09:30–10:06 on the time axis). Their labels show a fractional quantity and a stop "amount" that stays about the same (~660) on both the 16-tick and 34-tick boxes. He does not explain these labels; do not read them as his contract size. (11:50–14:15)
- P2.28 On-screen detail: for the news-day example he opens a ForexFactory calendar for Wed 14 Jan 2026 listing US PPI m/m, Core PPI m/m, Retail Sales m/m and Core Retail Sales m/m (the PPI rows are the ones he mentions). He gives no rule beyond halving the size. (14:19–14:45)
- P2.29 He says the fixed-contract approach spares him from panic and from keeping a calculator open. (14:41–15:01)

## P2.4 Funded (prop firm) accounts
- P2.30 Risk on funded accounts depends on the specific prop firm and account type; he says there is no single "one-stop" rule. (15:01–15:11)
- P2.31 His comparison (his description of the firms, not verified here): Alpha Futures has a somewhat larger drawdown and an easier consistency rule; Tradeify has a tighter consistency rule and a smaller drawdown but cheaper accounts. (15:11–15:45)
- P2.32 Drawdown types vary: trailing, trailing end-of-day, none, trailing equity; sizes vary too (his example: a $150,000 account with a $3,000 drawdown at one firm, $5,000 at another). (15:56–16:19)
- P2.33 Viewers must read the rules of their own account: the consistency rule (on the challenge and on payouts), whether drawdown trails equity or end of day, and how large it is. (16:29–17:02)
- P2.34 Challenge account and payout-eligible account call for different risk plans. (17:14–17:24)
- P2.35 What he says most people do: risk the maximum to pass the evaluation in as few trades as possible, then reduce risk once payout-eligible. His point: after passing, pull back and trade less risky because a payout is now possible. (17:24–18:06)
