## P6.1 What SMT divergence is
- P6.1 SMT divergence is about the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq only; he says forex and commodities viewers will get little from it. (0:00–0:21)
- P6.2 It means looking at two instruments at the same time, the S&P 500 (ES) and the Nasdaq (NQ), and comparing their highs and lows. (0:21–0:31)
- P6.3 Purpose: to help judge whether the trend is about to change before a break of structure or an inverse fair value gap is visible (both taught in earlier episodes of the series). (0:31–0:53)
- P6.4 It matters specifically when the divergence appears at a significant (higher-time-frame) draw on liquidity. (0:53–1:04)
- P6.5 He says he does not know what the letters SMT stand for (jokes "smart money transfer") and only reads "divergence" as a difference. (1:04–1:27)

## P6.2 Schematic example (bearish)
- P6.6 Set-up on a split screen: S&P 500 chart on the left, Nasdaq chart on the right, a higher-time-frame high marked as the draw on liquidity. (1:38–2:00)
- P6.7 On the S&P 500 price sweeps that high and then makes a LOWER high; he reads that as orders having been filled and the direction changing down. (2:00–2:25)
- P6.8 On the Nasdaq price makes a high and then a HIGHER high at the same time. The two are very correlated and trade alike, but their small differences can show what the other index will do. (2:25–3:08)
- P6.9 Reading: the S&P 500 shows that sell orders were filled; the Nasdaq is the more bullish pair and has not turned yet. (3:08–3:50)
- P6.10 If the S&P keeps falling, the Nasdaq is expected to follow, which can give a higher reward-to-risk trade on the Nasdaq or at least tell the Nasdaq's direction. (3:50–4:10)
- P6.11 Without the S&P, a higher high on the Nasdaq would look like a continuing uptrend; with it he treats the Nasdaq as possibly about to fall. (4:10–4:30)

## P6.3 When it is useful (context)
- P6.12 It is useful when draws on liquidity are being swept, because that is where orders can get filled. (4:41–4:52)
- P6.13 The two indexes are not perfectly correlated all the time, so context is needed. (4:52–5:05)
- P6.14 Away from sweeps of draws on liquidity these divergences appear all the time and are close to useless. (5:05–5:16)

## P6.4 Definitions — bearish and bullish
- P6.15 Bearish SMT divergence: one index makes a high then a LOWER high while the other index makes a high then a HIGHER high. It does not matter which index is which. (5:16–5:38)
- P6.16 It is a bearish confluence for BOTH indexes. The one with the lower high is the leading index of the move down; the one still making a higher high is the lagging index, which is expected to go down. (5:38–6:20)
- P6.17 Either index can play either role: if the Nasdaq makes the lower high and the S&P the higher high, then the Nasdaq leads and the S&P lags; still bearish for both. (6:20–6:51)
- P6.18 Bullish SMT divergence: in a downtrend, one index makes a low then a LOWER low (continuing the downtrend) while the other makes a low then a HIGHER low; again especially when taking out a significant draw on liquidity. (6:51–7:23)
- P6.19 The index making the higher low is the leading index (already starting an uptrend); the one making the lower low lags and is expected to move up; the leader gives an early look at what the lagger will do. (7:23–7:54)

## P6.5 How he uses it
- P6.20 Higher-time-frame example, bearish (4-hour, bias): an earlier high is swept by the Nasdaq, which makes a higher high; the S&P 500 stays under its high and makes a lower high. His reading: the Nasdaq swept orders, but overall price is bearish because the S&P made a lower high. (9:22–10:29)
- P6.21 What he would do on entry for that example: take the trade on the S&P 500, the leading and more bearish index; he would not short the bullish index (the Nasdaq), which pushed above the high. (10:29–11:09)
- P6.22 On a higher time frame it is mostly used for BIAS; he promises low-time-frame examples where it can be used for execution. (11:09–11:20)
- P6.23 After that bearish divergence both indexes dumped. (11:20–11:30)
- P6.24 Lower-time-frame example, bullish (5-minute): the S&P 500 made a low and then a higher low; at the same two points the Nasdaq made a low and then a LOWER low. Right after it both indexes traded higher. (12:40–13:22)
- P6.25 It happened while a higher-time-frame draw on liquidity was being swept. He says there was no complete "strategy entry" in that example, but it shows how one could look for an entry or use it as a confluence. (12:40–13:44)
- P6.26 Again he would take the trade on the leading index (the S&P 500 here, the more bullish one forming the new uptrend), not the lagging one that is still forming the downtrend. (13:33–14:05)
- P6.27 Sequence he describes: sweep a higher-time-frame draw, see a low and then a higher low on one index, look at the other and see a lower low = a very bullish confluence for both. (14:05–14:26)
- P6.28 Evidence he gives for taking the leader: the S&P 500 expanded bigger and faster, passing highs that the Nasdaq never got close to. (14:26–14:58)
- P6.29 He calls it a blend of confluences and says how it fits into the strategy comes later (strategy videos and trade recaps); for now the student should only identify it and watch reactions, especially at higher-time-frame draws on liquidity. (14:58–15:30)

## General definitions (web, not TJR)
- Smart-money divergence (SMT): a disagreement between correlated instruments, e.g. S&P 500 and Nasdaq, where one makes a higher high / lower low and the other fails to. Source: https://www.tradingview.com/ideas/smartmoneydivergence (trader education, not authoritative). TJR's own definition is P6.15–P6.19 above and wins.
