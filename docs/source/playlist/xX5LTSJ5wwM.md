## P10.1 What a fair value gap is
- P10.1 He uses "fair value gap" and "imbalance" for the same thing: an imbalance in price. (00:33–00:55)
- P10.2 Link to the liquidity episode: price is always either moving to take out liquidity or moving to fill an imbalance, which is why the concept matters. (00:12–00:33)
- P10.3 It is a three-candle pattern: one stretch of movement, then an expansionary (large, fast) candle, then another stretch of movement; the lack of orders sits between the first and the third candle. (00:55–01:17)
- P10.4 He says he does not trade classic candlestick patterns (head and shoulders, double top/bottom) because they do not explain why price moves; he wants a reason in order flow (orders filled after a liquidity sweep, a change in order flow, then continuation). A fair value gap is also three candles, but he frames it by the orders that are missing. (01:17–02:10)

## P10.2 Definition and how he draws it
- P10.5 He draws it with small trend lines on the candle wicks; only the top wick and the bottom wick matter, the other wicks do not. (02:20–02:46)
- P10.6 On the chart the gap is marked as a coloured rectangle covering the zone between the two wicks (green for a bullish gap, pink for a bearish gap), as seen on the frames; the caption wording is only "from the first candle's wick to the third candle's wick". (frames 04:27–05:39, 12:45, 14:25, 16:05)
- P10.7 Bullish fair value gap: first candle, then an expansionary candle that pushes price up strongly, then the third candle. (02:58–03:19)
- P10.8 The colour of the first and third candle does not matter (the first one may be a down candle or an up candle); only the middle candle's colour matters (up for bullish, down for bearish). (03:09–04:21, 07:16–07:38)
- P10.9 Condition for a bullish gap: a gap between the TOP wick of the first candle and the BOTTOM wick of the third candle. (04:11–04:31, 11:48–11:58, 28:09–28:31)
- P10.10 Bearish fair value gap: from the BOTTOM wick of the first candle to the TOP wick of the third candle, with a down expansionary candle in the middle, as long as there is a gap between the two. (07:16–08:10, 28:31–28:43)
- P10.11 Why it is an imbalance (bullish): buy orders pushed price up in the first candle, sell orders pushed it back down, and in the third candle sell orders only pushed price down to a level below which buyers took over again; the zone between the two wicks therefore has a lack of sell orders, shown by the expansionary candle crossing it quickly. (04:31–06:10)
- P10.12 Bearish mirror: sell orders pushed down and buy orders pushed back up in the first candle, buy orders only reached a certain height in the third; the zone between has a lack of buy orders. (08:10–08:52)
- P10.13 One-line summary: bullish = lack of sell orders inside a price range, bearish = lack of buy orders inside a price range. (09:13–09:33)

## P10.3 What is NOT a fair value gap
- P10.14 If the wicks of the first and third candle overlap there is no imbalance: orders on that side overlapped, so there is no lack of orders. Shown for both the bearish case (10:06–11:00) and the bullish case (11:00–11:48).

## P10.4 How it fits the strategy
- P10.15 Continuation confluence: in an uptrend price comes down into the gap; if buy orders then appear and push price up and out of it, the trend is expected to continue higher. (06:21–07:04)
- P10.16 Reasoning: since there is a lack of opposing orders in the gap, once orders are filled there, price has no hesitation to move on. Bearish mirror: in a downtrend, price pushes into the gap, sell orders appear, price goes lower. (06:10–06:21, 08:52–09:13)
- P10.17 The gap is useful in the current trend only; it is described as showing continuation of the trend you are already in. (16:44–16:54, 32:04–32:26)
- P10.18 Like every other confluence it cannot be traded on its own (not on a break of structure alone, not on a push above a high or below a low alone); the confluences are put together to build a strategy that gives probability. (09:33–10:06, 15:28–15:49)
- P10.19 Example, hourly: uptrend (high, low, higher high, higher low…), the higher low retraces into a bullish gap and price pushes higher out of it. (12:09–12:50)
- P10.20 Example, 15-minute, bearish: price sweeps a high (potential to fill orders), breaks structure down (confirmation that orders were filled), then a bearish gap acts as the continuation confluence; price pushes up into the gap, a bearish candle follows, and price continues lower for the time being. Frames: ES1! 15-minute chart, a green box on the swept high and a pink box on the bearish gap below it (13:45–14:45). (13:36–14:28)
- P10.21 Sequence he spells out in this example: liquidity sweep (potential for orders to be filled) -> break of structure (confirmation) -> fair value gap (continuation). This is the same step order as in clip #1 (P1.19). (13:36–14:18)

## P10.5 When the gap is disrespected (invalid)
- P10.22 A gap is disrespected when a candle CLOSES through it, similar to a break of structure: a bullish candle closing above a bearish gap means the gap is no longer valid or useful. (16:10–16:21)
- P10.23 Meaning: this typically means the trend has changed; since a gap is supposed to show continuation, a gap that is disrespected says the trend is disrespected. This hints at inverse fair value gaps, which he says come in a separate video and can be used as a confirmation confluence. (14:39–15:05, 16:21–16:54)
- P10.24 A wick that goes through the gap without a close beyond it has NOT disrespected it; a candle must close beyond the gap line, as with a break of structure. In the bullish example a wick went to the bottom and beyond but the close stayed in, so the gap held, and only a later large close below it disrespected it. (17:16–18:07)
- P10.25 Example, hourly, bearish: break of structure down, bearish gap, strong expansionary candle, price rises into the gap and a rejection candle forms, but a big bullish candle then closes above it, so the gap is disrespected. Used to prove that a down candle out of a gap is not a reason to press sell. (15:28–16:21)

## P10.6 Stacked gaps and when to remove a gap from the chart
- P10.26 Several gaps in a row with no retrace between them (no down candles in a bullish run): all of them are valid for price to push into. (18:17–19:12)
- P10.27 What "respected" looks like in his examples: price comes into the gap and a candle then closes back out in the direction of the trend (a bullish candle closing out of a bullish gap, a bearish candle out of a bearish gap); a wick into the gap followed by a bullish close out of it counts as price having balanced the imbalance. This is read in his examples, not stated as a rule with a name. (14:18–14:28, 23:09–24:01)
- P10.28 If price respects one and pushes above the high, all gaps underneath and the used one are removed: the whole move up was imbalanced price action, price came in, balanced it, and then went higher. (19:12–20:21)
- P10.29 If the top gap is closed under (not respected) this does not mean the trend changed, because the gaps below are still there; price can wick into a lower gap, then push above the high, and that is still valid. (20:21–21:23)
- P10.30 Uptrend example (price closed above a recent high): several stacked bullish gaps; price pushes through one, a wick goes under and into the next, then a bullish close out of it; no break of structure down, so still bullish structure; no need to reverse off the first gap because others sit below. Context and the other confluences are still required. (22:37–24:12)
- P10.31 Once price fills the gap(s) and then pushes above the high, the gap (and the one above) is deleted: price showed it did not need to fill it. He says not to drag it across the chart for price to hit it long afterwards (he jokes about three years). (24:12–25:07)
- P10.32 Bearish example: two consecutive bearish gaps; price retraces up into them in a downtrend, a bearish close under, then price pushes below the low; the imbalanced range is now balanced and the gaps are no longer needed. (25:37–26:32)
- P10.33 Common mistake he warns about: reading the retracement up as "filling the bearish gap" when in this case the move up was a sweep of highs; leaving used gaps on the chart or dragging old gaps across and shorting when price returns to them gets you stopped out. (26:32–27:49)
- P10.34 A gap that is not filled but price retraces and then pushes above the high in the uptrend: price had no obligation to fill it; as with liquidity, a push above a high only gives the opportunity to reverse, it does not force one; delete the gap. (28:53–30:40, 30:40–31:13)
- P10.35 Weekend example: two gaps, a retrace, then a much higher open and a continuation of the uptrend; both gaps are removed and not dragged across, because they apply only inside the current trend. (31:13–32:26)
- P10.36 Bearish stacked pair, no retrace between them: price comes up and balances both, then a close under the low; both are removed; if kept, you will wrongly expect sell orders out of them. (32:26–33:18)
- P10.37 The next episode is on advanced imbalance concepts or on inverse fair value gaps (he is not sure which comes next); imbalances continue tomorrow. How stacked gaps work with inverse gaps is deferred. (27:49–28:09, 33:18–33:41)
