## P9.2 New day opening gap
- P9.5 Definition: the market is closed for about one hour between the close of one day and the open of the next; if the new day opens away from the previous day's close, the space between close and open is a new day opening gap. (01:04–01:26, 02:33–02:45)
- P9.6 It is an area of imbalance that price actively wants to seek out and balance before continuing in the direction it wants to go. (01:36–01:47, 02:33–02:57)
- P9.7 It can be treated as a very strong draw on liquidity. He pre-empts the objection that imbalances are not draws: fair value gaps are not required to be filled (from the previous episode), and neither are opening gaps, but price has a high probability, more often than not, of seeking them out. (02:57–03:31)
- P9.8 Two ways to use it: as an entry (price is expected to go to the gap first before continuing the trend, so look for an entry there), or as a target (when price is well above or away from the gap, it is probably where price will draw to). (03:31–03:51)
- P9.9 Why it counts as a real gap: there is one hour with no trading in which price can shift. (05:58–06:10)
- P9.10 It is rarely tradable: he says it is rare to be able to take trades towards new day or new week opening gaps; the day-gap example sits in the Asian session, where he is not looking to trade, so it is hit or miss. (01:47–01:59, 08:10–08:22)
- P9.11 Chart example (a daily candle): the day closes low, the new day opens above it, and the gap is filled completely within the new day. On the 4-hour, the 1-hour and the 5-minute he shows the exact moment: the hour skipped is 17:00 (the 16:00 candle is the close, the next candle's open is the gap), and price fills the gap immediately when the new day opens. (07:03–07:57) Frames: ES1! (S&P 500 E-mini) on daily, then 4h, then 1h, then 5m; on the 1h, horizontal lines mark the two ends of the gap. ## P9.3 New week opening gap
- P9.12 Definition: the market closes Friday and reopens Sunday afternoon (time depends on the time zone); a gap between Friday's close and Sunday's open is a new week opening gap, the same idea as the day gap on weekly candles. (03:51–04:23)
- P9.13 Price will typically seek out this weekly imbalance and then continue in its direction. Uses: trades towards the gap as a target, or trades inside the gap as an entry. (04:12–04:44)
- P9.14 Why it counts as a real gap: there are two whole days in which price can shift. (06:10–06:21)
- P9.15 Why it gives more opportunity than the day gap: weekly gaps are typically larger and more drastic, creating more of a range to trade into. Sunday trading is often stagnant because no new US money has entered yet, so Monday offers a chance to trade towards the gap or out of it. (08:10–08:54)
- P9.16 Example 1 (week of Monday 20 October): the week opens far above the previous weekly close, a huge gap. On the daily, price traded up from Sunday's open, took a liquidity sweep around Wednesday, then went down to fill the gap, which served as the target. (08:54–09:59)
- P9.17 Example 2: price opens on Sunday, immediately trades down, fills the gap and continues higher; here the gap was an entry point, but trading on a Sunday is unlikely. (09:59–10:31)
- P9.18 If a gap is not filled right away (he says price wants to fill gaps as fast as possible), then coming into Monday's open it is a very good target. (10:31–10:56)

## P9.4 New candle opening gap
- P9.19 Definition: the same as the day and week gaps but on any time frame; example: a 4-hour candle closes and the next 4-hour candle opens higher, leaving an imbalance that price wants to balance before continuing. (04:44–05:26)
- P9.20 He calls it nearly useless and says one would almost never trade off it, as an entry or as a target. (05:26–05:47, 11:49–12:10)
- P9.21 Reason: with the market open and orders flowing, a candle rarely jumps from one close to the next open, and there is no skipped time (unlike one hour for the day gap and two days for the week gap). (05:47–06:10, 06:21–06:53)
- P9.22 Chart examples on a 1-minute chart: tiny gaps between consecutive candles are filled straight after the candle closes, then price continues a little lower. He stresses they are microscopic. (11:07–11:49)

## P9.5 BPR — definition
- P9.23 BPR = two imbalanced price ranges: a swift move down (or up) through an imbalance, followed by a swift move back up (or down) through imbalanced price. The whole range is then identified as imbalanced price. (12:10–12:32, 16:36–16:57)
- P9.24 He also describes it as an illiquid price range in which a swift move up and a swift move down are both visible. (15:53–16:05)
- P9.25 Why imbalance exists: in the bullish case there was a lack of sell orders (or just a lack of orders), so price rips through it with the buy orders filled. (14:09–14:36)
- P9.26 Core rule: after a big swift move straight up or down, when price re-enters that range it can fill the range completely and also move down through it as swiftly as it moved up. (14:36–14:59)

## P9.6 BPR — how it is used
- P9.27 As a target: once price re-enters the range, it moves swiftly to the bottom of the range created by the swift move up. (16:57–17:09)
- P9.28 As an entry: once price has filled back into the big range, look for continuation out of it, shown as a break of structure to the upside, a retrace, then price continues higher. (17:09–17:31)
- P9.29 His own trade (Tuesday, Nasdaq short, 10:41): he identified an illiquid range after a rapid move up, waited for bearish confirmation from price inside it, and targeted the bottom of that move, expecting price to pass down through the range as smoothly as it came up and then continue in the original direction. - P9.30 Confirmation use: after price comes up and balances an imbalance, look for price to come back down to balance the other one; or find bullish confirmation inside the range to take a trade up. How to combine this with other confluences into a strategy is deferred. (19:36–20:30)
