## F1 What liquidity is
- F1.1 Liquidity = open/resting orders on the chart. (00:45–01:30)
- F1.2 TJR calls liquidity "cookies": the market needs it to function, like people need cookies. (01:00)
- F1.3 "Every single trade I make is based on liquidity. The market cannot move without liquidity." (01:30)
- F1.4 Liquidity sweep = that liquidity is removed; sleeping orders get activated and the market fills buy or sell orders. (01:30)
- F1.5 It happens at smart-money level (big money, algorithms, hedge funds — those who move the market) and at retail level (us). Retail liquidity and smart-money liquidity are two different things; you must know where each sits. (01:45–02:15)
- F1.6 Core statement: liquidity sits above highs and below lows. It really is that simple; people overcomplicate it. "Complex does not mean better." (02:15–03:15, 04:30)

## F2 Identifying highs and lows (05:15)
- F2.1 A high = an up move followed by a down move. A two-candle pattern: an up (bullish) candle then a down (bearish) candle. The high is the highest point (highest wick) of those two candles.
- F2.2 A low = a down candle followed by an up candle. The low is the lowest point (lowest wick) of those two candles.
- F2.3 "Believe it or not, some people mix this up." (05:45)

## F3 Why liquidity sits above highs / below lows — retail liquidity (06:00–10:30)
- F3.1 Uptrend = higher highs and higher lows. Downtrend = lower highs and lower lows. (06:15)
- F3.2 In an uptrend, if the market wants to reverse, it can only reverse above a high — the current high or another high from earlier price action. It must go up and take a high. (06:45–07:15)
- F3.3 Above highs sit two kinds of retail BUY orders: (a) traders who press buy when price breaks above the high, expecting the uptrend to continue; (b) traders who shorted on the pullback, whose stop-losses sit above the high (a short's stop is a buy). (07:30–09:45)
- F3.4 Below lows sit two kinds of retail SELL orders: (a) traders who short the break of a low in a downtrend; (b) traders who bought the pullback expecting a reversal, whose stop-losses sit below the low. (09:45–10:30)
- F3.5 "In an uptrend there are two kinds of buy orders above highs; in a downtrend two kinds of sell orders below lows. That is retail liquidity." (10:30)
- F3.6 "99% of traders fail"; trading like retail gets you taken advantage of. (10:30)

## F4 Smart-money liquidity (11:15–16:30)
- F4.1 Old-school Apple share story: to buy, someone must be willing to sell to you. Brokers digitised this, but you still need a counterparty. (11:15–12:45)
- F4.2 A market mover who wants to push price down needs a huge amount of orders filled; they need retail to enter in the opposite direction. (12:45–13:30)
- F4.3 Breaking above a high triggers both retail buy groups → gives market makers the opportunity to fill massive SELL orders and push price the other way. (13:30–14:15)
- F4.4 Mirror: breaking below a low triggers retail sells (breakdown shorts + stopped-out longs) → opportunity to fill massive BUY orders. (15:00–15:45)
- F4.5 "We want to trade with the big guys / on the side of smart money; we want to trade the reversal points." (14:30, 15:45)
- F4.6 IMPORTANT: breaking a high does NOT mean market makers always reverse — it gives them the OPPORTUNITY. Other confluences (taught on his channel) tell you when they actually take it. Today's video is only liquidity. (15:45–16:30)
- F4.7 It happens on every timeframe: daily, 4-hour… To go from downtrend to uptrend the market must sweep liquidity (a low) then go higher; uptrend to downtrend: take a high then go lower. (16:30–17:15)

## F8 Low-resistance vs high-resistance liquidity (39:45–48:00)
- F8.1 Low-resistance liquidity = a series of stacked highs or lows that have NOT been taken yet ("a pile of cookies"). Some call it trendline liquidity. (39:45–40:45)
- F8.2 Market makers see stacked highs as many sets of buy orders that trigger when price pushes above — imagine the sell orders they can fill; they actively seek and take them. Same for stacked lows. (40:45–42:00)
- F8.3 Chart example: trendline liquidity — you can literally draw a trendline under lows that were not taken; price actively runs them, fills buys, and goes higher. (42:00–42:45)
- F8.4 Liquidity targets can also act as PRICE TARGETS: positions entered below must be closed where people are willing to buy — above stacked highs. So price often actively runs stacked highs to take profit. (42:45–44:15)
- F8.5 Example: a few stacked highs not taken → price pushes up, "thanks for the low-resistance liquidity", then pushes down. Stacked lows → price comes down and can turn up from there. (44:15–45:00)
- F8.6 High-resistance liquidity = the most recent high or a liquidity sweep that price is currently moving away from. Once low-resistance liquidity is taken and price reverses cleanly, it becomes high-resistance: it would take a lot for price to go back up there. Market makers would not sweep highs, push down, and come back up to the same highs — they need to go down to lows to take profit. (45:00–46:30)
- F8.7 Rule: trade toward low-resistance liquidity, away from high-resistance liquidity. After highs are taken and price tends lower, it must first take lows before we even consider longs to target those highs. (46:30–47:30)
- F8.8 Final example: stacked highs above; price comes down, takes lows, confirms higher → that low is now high-resistance; trade toward the low-resistance (stacked) highs. (47:15–48:00)
